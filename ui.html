audio engine: idle —
Ricoh RP2A03  ·  5-channel APU  ·  NTSC 1.789773 MHz  ·  1983

Nobody ever recorded this music.

There is no audio file inside a Super Mario Bros. cartridge. There is a program that writes eleven bytes into a sound chip, sixty times a second, and the chip builds the waveform out of nothing but integers and a clock divider.

Below is that whole chain, working. The tune is an original written in the same idiom — same chip, same limits, same four voices — so you can take it apart without taking apart somebody's copyright. Press play, then follow the signal down to the speaker cone.

01

A note is an integer, and it is slightly wrong

Music data → APU registers

The chip cannot be told "play A above middle C." It has one clock running at 1,789,773 Hz and an 11-bit counter. You give it a divider, it gives you a pitch:

f = 1789773 / ( 16 × ( t + 1 ) )

Since t must be a whole number, most notes are unreachable. The composer gets the nearest available one. Look at the error column: in the bass the grid is fine enough to be invisible, but the higher you go the coarser it gets, because the same integer step covers more and more Hz. That drift is part of the sound you remember.

02

Four voices, and you may not have a fifth

Registers → digital waveform

Every NES soundtrack ever written is these channels. Not "up to" these channels — exactly these, always, with fixed jobs. Melody and harmony fight over two pulse channels, the bass gets the triangle whether it suits the song or not, and all percussion is one noise generator.

On: every solo is scaled by the full mix's peak, so you hear the real balance — the triangle is genuinely the loudest channel. Off: each solo is normalised to itself, like the shipped WAVs.
Pulse channel 1 — duty select 50% · fat
CH.1   1 ms/div

Two bits. Four settings, and 75% sounds identical to 25% — so in practice, three tone colours for the entire console. Everything you think of as an NES "instrument" is one of these three, plus a volume envelope.

The trick composers used to cheat

A pulse channel plays one note. It cannot play a chord. So drivers flip the pitch register every single frame — C, E, G, C, E, G, sixty times a second — fast enough that your ear fuses it into a chord instead of hearing three separate notes. It buzzes, and that buzz became a genre signature.

03

The mixer is four resistors, so it lies

Waveform → voltage

Each channel produces a number from 0 to 15 — four bits, not eight. Those numbers do not get added. They drive a resistor ladder into a single output pin, and a resistor ladder is not linear: the louder things get, the more the chip compresses them on its own.

Nobody designed that as an effect. It is a consequence of cheap 1983 analogue parts, and it is a real part of why the console sounds glued together rather than like four things playing at once.

04

The speaker cannot draw a corner

Voltage → moving air

The chip hands the amplifier a waveform with perfectly vertical edges. The speaker cannot reproduce it, and that is not a defect — it is mass. A cone is a weight on a spring pushed by a coil, so its motion obeys

m·x″  +  c·x′  +  k·x  =  B·l·i(t)

which is a low-pass filter wearing physics notation. Feed it a square wave and it overshoots, rings at its own resonance, and rounds every edge. Two consequences follow. A tiny speaker resonates high — around 650 Hz here — so it physically cannot move far enough to make bass, which is why the triangle channel's bassline nearly vanishes on a small speaker and returns on headphones. And the sharpness you hear in a square wave is its harmonics: the corner is where they live, so as the cone rounds the corner it is also softening the tone.

Drag toward 1600 Hz for a phone earpiece and the low end evaporates. Drag toward 120 Hz for a large cabinet and the wave arrives almost intact.

05

So what does "8-bit" actually mean?

The part that was genuinely clever

Not the audio. The audio is 4-bit, and it isn't sampled at all. "8-bit" describes the CPU — the console's era, not its sound depth. The name stuck to the music because the music is inseparable from the machine that computes it.

Here is the actual advance, in the only unit that mattered in 1985. A cartridge had tens of kilobytes of ROM for everything — levels, sprites, code. Storing recorded audio was not slightly too expensive; it was off by three orders of magnitude:

So the music had to be an algorithm, and the composer's job became writing for a synthesiser he could not modify, with three timbres and no chords. Koji Kondo's melodies are memorable partly because they had to survive being played on a square wave with a hole in the middle of the arrangement — everything decorative was unaffordable, so only the hook was left.

That is the gist. The constraint did the composing as much as the composer did.
